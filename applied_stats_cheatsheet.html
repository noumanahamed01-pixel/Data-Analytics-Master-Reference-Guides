Applied Statistics Cheat Sheet

Business-first reference for data analysts moving toward machine learning. Every concept is tied to a real business problem. Skip the academic proofs — focus on what to use, when, and what breaks if you use it wrong.

01

Descriptive Statistics

Central Tendency — Mean, Median, Mode
ML Signal Baseline
MEAN
Sum ÷ count. Sensitive to outliers — one extreme value shifts it.
Use when: data is symmetric, no extreme outliers.
MEDIAN
Middle value when sorted. Robust to outliers. Always prefer for skewed data.
Use when: income, price, time-to-event data.
MODE
Most frequent value. The only central metric for categorical data.
Use when: category imputation, retail product demand.
Business Problem — E-commerce

Your average order value (AOV) is ₹1,840 — but median order is ₹620. Mean is inflated by a handful of bulk B2B orders. Report median to ops, use mean only for revenue forecasting where the outliers are real and recurring.

Analyst Tip

Before feeding a feature to a model, always compare mean vs. median. If they diverge by more than 20%, your feature is skewed and likely needs a log transform or outlier treatment before training.

Spread — Variance, Standard Deviation, IQR
Outlier Detection Feature Scaling
σ² = Σ(xᵢ - μ)² / N # Variance — average squared distance from mean σ = √σ² # Std Dev — same unit as your data IQR = Q3 - Q1 # Interquartile range — middle 50% of data Outlier threshold: x < Q1 - 1.5×IQR or x > Q3 + 1.5×IQR
Business Problem — Logistics

Delivery time has σ = 2.1 days. Operations wants a "reliable" SLA. Set it at mean + 2σ and you cover ~97.5% of deliveries. High std dev also signals your model needs delivery_region as a feature — some routes are inherently unpredictable.

Analyst Tip

Low variance in a feature = low predictive value. A column where 95% of rows have the same value adds almost no signal to a model. Flag and potentially drop it before training.

Skewness & Kurtosis — Shape of Distribution
Transform Signal ML Readiness
Skewness > 1 or < -1 → heavily skewed, consider log/sqrt transform Kurtosis > 3 → heavy tails, more extreme outliers than normal
Right Skew (positive)
Income, price, transaction value, session length
Long tail on the right. Mean > median. Apply log(x + 1) transform before using in linear models.
Left Skew (negative)
Test scores, customer satisfaction, age at retirement
Long tail on the left. Median > mean. Reflect then log-transform, or use quantile binning.
Do Not Ignore This

Linear regression assumes normally distributed residuals — not normally distributed features. But highly skewed features distort the residuals anyway. Always check skewness of key numeric features before modeling, not after.

Percentiles & Z-Scores — Contextualizing a Number
Normalization Benchmarking
Z = (x - μ) / σ # How many std deviations from the mean? |Z| > 3 → likely outlier Percentile rank → "this customer spends more than 87% of your base"
Business Problem — SaaS / Product Analytics

A user's session time is 42 minutes. Alone that's meaningless. Z-score of +2.3 means they're in the top ~1% of engaged users — that's your power user segment. This is how you build a "high-engagement" binary feature for a churn model.

Analyst Tip

Z-score normalization (standardization) is required before feeding numeric features to distance-based models like KNN or SVM, and before PCA. Tree-based models (Random Forest, XGBoost) do not require it — they split on rank, not magnitude.

02

Probability Distributions — Business Use Cases

You don't need to derive these. You need to recognize which one fits your data and what that tells you about the business problem — and which ML models assume which distribution.

Normal (Gaussian)
Height, measurement error, aggregated financial returns
Bell curve. Mean = median = mode. Most linear models assume your residuals follow this. Central Limit Theorem makes averages of large samples normal even if the underlying data isn't.
Linear Regression · t-tests · ANOVA
Log-Normal
Revenue, transaction value, time-on-site, stock prices
Right-skewed; log transform makes it normal. If your revenue data looks like a hockey stick, it's log-normal. Log(revenue) is often a better model feature than raw revenue.
Apply log transform before linear models
Binomial
Click / no-click, converted / not, churned / retained
n independent trials, each either success or failure. Foundation for A/B testing on conversion rates. Logistic regression is the modeling equivalent — it models the probability of the binary outcome.
Logistic Regression · A/B Test
Poisson
Support tickets/hour, orders/day, defects/batch, app crashes
Count of events in a fixed interval when events are rare and independent. Mean = variance. If variance >> mean, you have overdispersion — use Negative Binomial instead.
Count Regression · Anomaly Detection
Exponential
Time between customer purchases, time until churn, machine failure intervals
Time between Poisson events. Memoryless — the probability of the next event doesn't depend on how long you've already waited. Foundation for survival analysis / time-to-event models.
Survival Analysis · Lifetime Value
Uniform
Random assignment in experiments, hash-based user bucketing
All values equally likely. Used to assign users to A/B groups. If your "random" assignment isn't uniform, your test is biased from day one — always verify group balance before analyzing results.
Experiment Design · Sampling
Analyst Tip — Distribution Fingerprinting

In Python: df['col'].hist(bins=50) gives you the shape. Then scipy.stats.normaltest() tests normality formally. If p < 0.05, the data is not normal — transform or pick the right model before proceeding.

03

A/B Testing Framework

The 7-Step A/B Test Process — Never Skip a Step
Experimentation
01
Define Metric
One primary metric. Conversion rate, revenue/user, retention D7. Don't change it mid-test.
02
State Hypothesis
H₀: no difference. H₁: variant B has higher conversion. Be directional if you have a reason.
03
Set α & Power
α = 0.05 (5% false positive risk). Power = 0.80 (80% chance to detect a real effect).
04
Calculate Sample Size
Use a power calculator. Smaller expected effect = more users needed. Do not skip this.
05
Run the Test
Random assignment. Do not peek at results before hitting target sample size.
06
Analyze
t-test for means, z-test for proportions. Compute p-value and confidence interval.
07
Decide & Ship
p < α and effect is practically significant? Ship B. Never ship on statistical significance alone.
Business Problem — Fintech App

You want to test a new onboarding flow. Primary metric: 7-day activation rate. Baseline: 34%. Minimum detectable effect: +3 percentage points. At 80% power, α=0.05 → you need ~2,400 users per group. Run for full weeks (not days) to avoid day-of-week bias. After 14 days: p=0.031, new flow = 37.4% activation. Ship it — but also check if the effect holds for mobile vs. desktop segments.

The Peeking Problem

Checking results daily and stopping when p < 0.05 inflates your false positive rate from 5% to ~40% over 20 peeks. Commit to a stopping rule before the experiment starts, or use Sequential testing / Bayesian methods if you need early stopping.

⚠ The Multiple Comparisons Problem — One Test, One Metric
Watch OutPM Pressure

Every time you run an additional significance test at α = 0.05, you accept a 5% chance of a false positive. Run 20 tests and you're near-certain to find at least one "significant" result purely by chance.

P(at least one false positive) = 1 - (1 - α)^n n = 20 tests at α = 0.05 → 1 - (0.95)²⁰ = 64% false positive risk
Business Problem — Product A/B Test

Your PM asks you to check 20 secondary metrics after the experiment: clicks, scroll depth, time on page, hover rate, share rate... You find that "average scroll depth" is significant at p = 0.03. Congratulations — that is almost certainly noise. With 20 metrics tested, you expect one false positive just from luck, and scroll depth was never in your pre-registered hypothesis. The fix: declare your primary metric and 2–3 guardrail metrics before the test runs. Everything else is exploratory, not conclusive.

Analyst Tip — How to Handle It

Bonferroni correction: divide your α threshold by the number of tests. Testing 5 metrics? Use α = 0.01 per test instead of 0.05. More conservative but simple. Alternatively, use the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the False Discovery Rate — less punishing than Bonferroni and more appropriate when you're doing exploratory metric sweeps.

04

Hypothesis Testing — p-values Done Right

p-value — What It Actually Means
Most Misused Concept
p-value = P(observed data | H₀ is true) NOT: probability that H₀ is true NOT: probability the result happened by chance YES: how surprising is this result, assuming nothing is going on?
  • p < 0.05: if H₀ were true, you'd see a result this extreme less than 5% of the time. Reject H₀.
  • p = 0.03: statistically significant. Does NOT mean the effect is large or business-relevant.
  • p = 0.20: insufficient evidence to reject H₀. Does NOT prove H₀ is true.
Business Problem — Retail Pricing

You test a price discount. p = 0.002 (very significant). Lift in conversion: +0.4%. With 50,000 daily users, that's 200 more conversions — but the discount costs ₹15/conversion in margin. Net: −₹3,000/day. Statistical significance without business significance = a wrong decision. Always report effect size and confidence interval alongside p-value.

Analyst Tip — Report This Instead

Never report just the p-value. Always show: (1) effect size (the actual difference), (2) 95% confidence interval, (3) practical significance (does this move a business metric meaningfully?). A p-value is a gate — not an answer.

05

t-Tests & ANOVA — Choosing the Right Test

Test When to Use Business Example Key Assumption
One-sample t-test Compare one group's mean to a fixed benchmark "Is our avg delivery time significantly different from the 3-day SLA?" Approx. normal distribution or n > 30
Two-sample t-test Compare means of two independent groups "Does Group A (new email) spend more than Group B (control)?" Independence, approx. normal, similar variance (check with Levene's test)
Paired t-test Same subjects measured twice (before/after) "Did our training program increase sales rep output, comparing each rep to themselves?" Differences between pairs are normally distributed
One-way ANOVA Compare means across 3+ independent groups "Which of our 4 pricing tiers drives highest avg order value?" Normality per group, equal variance (Bartlett's test), independence
Two-way ANOVA Two categorical factors + their interaction "Does the effect of discount size on conversion differ by customer segment?" Same as one-way, plus balanced design is preferred
Welch's t-test Two groups with unequal variances A/B test where control group is 10× larger than variant (unequal n) More robust than Student's t — default to this for most A/B tests
ANOVA — What Significance Actually Tells You
Common Mistake
F = Variance between groups / Variance within groups F >> 1 → group means differ more than expected by chance p < 0.05 → at least one group is different. But which one?
ANOVA Does Not Tell You Which Groups Differ

A significant ANOVA F-test only tells you some group is different. You need a post-hoc test to find which pairs. Use Tukey's HSD for all pairwise comparisons, or Bonferroni correction when you have specific hypotheses — but you must plan which comparisons before seeing the data, not after.

Business Problem — Marketing Channels

You have 4 acquisition channels (paid search, organic, referral, direct). ANOVA on LTV: p = 0.003. Tukey HSD reveals: referral vs. direct is the only significant pair (referral LTV is +₹4,200 higher). Paid search and organic are not meaningfully different. Now you know where to reallocate budget — without ANOVA followed by post-hoc, you'd have reacted to noise.

Correlation vs. Causation

Correlation Coefficients — Reading the Number
Feature Selection
Pearson r → linear relationship between two continuous variables (-1 to +1) Spearman ρ → rank-based, works for non-linear monotonic relationships Kendall τ → rank concordance, better for small samples and many ties |r| < 0.1 : negligible |r| 0.1–0.3 : weak |r| 0.3–0.5 : moderate |r| > 0.5 : strong
Business Problem — HR Analytics

Pearson r between "hours of training" and "performance score" = 0.62. Strong positive correlation. But before recommending more training: check if top performers are simply assigned more training (reverse causality), or if a third variable (department, manager quality) drives both. Correlation finds the signal. Causation requires design or domain logic.

Analyst Tip — Correlation for Feature Selection

Features with |r| > 0.9 to each other are multicollinear — they're measuring the same thing. Drop or combine them before linear regression (use VIF — Variance Inflation Factor — to formally test). Tree-based models are less sensitive to this but still wasteful. Target correlation: features with |r| ≈ 0.3–0.7 to the target variable are your best bets for signal.

Causation Frameworks — When Correlation Isn't Enough
Decision Making
MethodBusiness ContextUse When
A/B Test (RCT) Product changes, pricing, marketing copy You can randomly assign users. Gold standard for causation.
Difference-in-Differences Policy rollout, regional promotions Treatment rolled out to one group, not another. Compare change in both.
Regression Discontinuity Loyalty tier upgrades, credit score cutoffs Eligibility determined by threshold — compare just above vs. just below.
Instrumental Variables Pricing strategy, ad exposure effects Use an external variable that affects treatment but not outcome directly.
The Classic Business Trap

"Users who engage with 5+ features have 3× lower churn." This is correlation — engaged users were already committed. Forcing all users to engage with 5 features does not cause lower churn. Always ask: is this driven by selection bias? What type of user self-selects into this behavior?

Simpson's Paradox — When Aggregates Lie
Dangerous Pattern Segmentation

A trend appears in every individual subgroup of your data — but reverses or disappears when you look at the combined total. This is Simpson's Paradox, and it is one of the most common ways that correct-looking aggregate analyses lead to wrong business decisions.

Business Problem — E-commerce Conversion

Overall site conversion rate dropped from 4.2% to 3.8% month-over-month. The team panics. But segment it: Mobile conversion went up (3.1% → 3.4%) and Desktop conversion also went up (6.8% → 7.1%). Both channels improved. The aggregate fell because the traffic mix shifted — mobile visits grew from 55% to 72% of total traffic, and mobile converts at a lower absolute rate than desktop. The product got better. The channel mix changed. Reporting only the aggregate would have triggered a wrong investigation.

# Simulated example — mix shift causing aggregate reversal Month 1: Mobile 55% of traffic × 3.1% CVR + Desktop 45% × 6.8% CVR = 4.2% overall Month 2: Mobile 72% of traffic × 3.4% CVR + Desktop 28% × 7.1% CVR = 3.8% overall Both segments improved. Aggregate fell. The mix is the story.
Analyst Tip — How to Catch It

Whenever you report a change in an aggregate metric (conversion, churn, AOV, NPS), immediately segment by the key stratifying variable — device, channel, geography, cohort, pricing tier. If the aggregate tells a different story than the segments, you have a mix-shift problem, not a performance problem. In SQL: always GROUP BY segment before you trust a GROUP BY month.

Where It Appears Most

Medical trial results aggregated across hospitals with different patient severity profiles. Marketing ROI aggregated across channels with different deal sizes. HR diversity metrics aggregated across departments with different hiring volumes. Any time you aggregate across groups with different underlying compositions, Simpson's Paradox is a risk. Segment first, aggregate second.

Transformations — Fixing Skewed Features
ML ReadinessTransform
SituationTransformBusiness Column Example

Analyst Tip

Fit scalers on training data ONLY. Apply the same fitted scaler to validation and test sets. If you fit on the full dataset, you've leaked test information into the training process — your reported accuracy is optimistic and your model will underperform in production.

Pre-Model Assumption Checklist

Run through every item below before feeding transformed data into an algorithm. Check off each one as you verify it. A model trained on unchecked data is a model waiting to fail in production.

The Class Imbalance Metric Trap — Accuracy Is Lying to You
Most Dangerous Mistake Classification Models

When your target class is rare — fraud, disease, churn in a low-churn product, equipment failure — accuracy becomes a useless metric that actively hides a broken model.

# Fraud detection dataset: 99% legitimate, 1% fraud Model A: predicts "Not Fraud" for every single transaction Accuracy = 99% ← looks great. catches zero fraud. Model B: catches 80% of fraud, misclassifies 5% of legitimate Accuracy = 94.2% ← looks worse. actually useful.
MetricWhat It AnswersUse When
Precision Of all predicted positives, how many are actually positive? False positives are costly — e.g., wrongly flagging a customer as fraudulent and blocking their card
Recall (Sensitivity) Of all actual positives, how many did the model find? False negatives are costly — e.g., missing a real fraud case or failing to detect a disease
F1 Score Harmonic mean of Precision and Recall You need to balance both — no strong business reason to favour one error type
AUC-ROC How well does the model rank positives above negatives across all thresholds? Comparing models; works well unless imbalance is severe (>100:1), then use PR-AUC
Accuracy Overall fraction correct Only when classes are roughly balanced (less than 3:1 ratio)
Business Problem — Insurance Fraud Detection

Your fraud model hits 98.7% accuracy. The product team is thrilled. Then you check: your fraud recall is 11% — you're catching barely 1 in 10 actual fraud cases. The model learned to say "not fraud" almost always, because that's the 98.7% base rate. Fix: switch your evaluation metric to F1 or PR-AUC, use class_weight='balanced' in sklearn, and consider SMOTE to oversample the minority class before training.

Analyst Tip — The Threshold Question

Classification models output a probability, not a hard label. The 0.5 decision threshold is an arbitrary default. For a fraud model, lowering the threshold to 0.3 catches more fraud (higher recall) at the cost of more false alarms (lower precision). Plot the Precision-Recall curve and pick the threshold based on the business cost ratio of a missed fraud vs. a false positive — don't let sklearn's default choose it for you.
